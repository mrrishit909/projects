Experiment readout · MineThatData e-mail test · 64,000 customers · 2-week window

The men's e-mail won

Customers who bought in the last year were split at random into three groups: a men's merchandise e-mail, a women's merchandise e-mail, or no e-mail. Two weeks later, did the e-mails change what they did?

Recommendation

Send the men's e-mail. It raised spend by $0.77 per customer (95% CI $0.49 to $1.05), about $769 more two-week revenue per 1,000 customers e-mailed, vs $424 for the women's e-mail. That is revenue, not profit: the e-mail's cost and margins aren't in the data. Next, test the women's e-mail only on customers who have bought women's merchandise (see section 4).

1 · Was the test fair?

Yes. Group sizes were 21,307, 21,387, 21,306: what a random one-third split produces (sample-ratio test p = 0.90). Across 18 things known before the e-mail (recency, past spend, channel, area, new customer…), the groups differ by at most 0.016 standard deviations; 0.1 is the usual warning line. So differences after the e-mail are caused by the e-mail.

3 · Men's vs women's e-mail, head to head

The men's e-mail beat the women's on every metric: spend +$0.35 per customer (95% CI +$0.03 to +$0.66, p = 0.030 after Holm), visits +3.1 pts, purchases +0.37 pts. One caution on spend: with ~21,306 customers per group the test had only 58% power for a gap that size (35,761 per group would give 80%). Gaps that clear the bar in under-powered tests tend to be overstated, so the true difference is probably smaller than $0.35.

4 · Who responds? (exploratory)

Men's e-mailWomen's e-mail

The men's e-mail lifted visits in every group. The women's e-mail barely moved customers who had only ever bought men's merchandise (+1.1 pts) but worked as well as the men's e-mail on women's-only buyers (+7.4 pts). That gap (+6.3 pts, p < 0.001) was spotted after looking at this table, so it's a lead for the next test, not a finding. The one segment test chosen in advance (does the men's e-mail's spend effect differ between men's-only and women's-only buyers?) found no difference (+$0.07, p = 0.81).

5 · Could the tests be fooling us?

Each method was run 1,000 times on two random halves of the no-e-mail group, where nothing differs. A sound test calls about 5% of those "significant". Visits: 4.9%. Spend: 5.2%, so Welch's t holds up despite the long tail. Purchases: 6.8% with the z-test, a little hot because buyers are rare (about 61 per half), and 4.7% with Fisher's exact test. Both purchase effects in section 2 also pass Fisher's test (larger p = 0.0002).

Smallest effect this design could reliably catch (80% power): +0.8 pts on visits (8% of the 10.6% base), +0.20 pts on purchases (36% of 0.57%), $0.31 on spend (48% of $0.65).

What this test can't tell us

  • Profit: e-mail cost, margins and returns aren't in the data.
  • Anything after two weeks, including unsubscribes or e-mail fatigue from sending every week.
  • Today's customers: the data is from 2008 (released by Kevin Hillstrom for the MineThatData challenge).
